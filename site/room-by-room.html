Room-by-Room Walkthrough

Read the guide first, then walk your actual home with the Walk-through Checklist.

The point of this walkthrough is not to feel bad about your house. Every home has weak spots; we're just finding yours and putting them in piles labeled "this weekend," "this month," and "someday."

How to do the walk: Take notes on paper or an index card. Walk slowly, in the shoes you actually wear at home (not your "nice" shoes). Ask yourself one question in every room: If I was tired, rushing, or carrying something, where would this room trip me up?

Start at the street

Independence starts outside. If getting in the door is hard, everything after it is harder.

  • Path and steps: Cracked or uneven walkway? Loose handrail? Poor lighting at the door at night? Motion-sensor lights by the entry are one of the best money-for-safety trades in this whole guide.
  • The front door: Does it need two hands to open? Deadbolt keyhole at an awkward height? Consider a quality smart lock (keypad or keyed) — but choose one with a physical key backup and check the door still latches securely.
  • Stairs to the entry: Every step should have a handrail. Stair edges in contrast color (paint or hazard tape) help eyes that no longer read dark treads well.
  • A bench near the door: Somewhere to set down groceries while you find keys. A wooden deck box works too.

Entry hall and main floor hallway

  • Clear the runway. Shoes, cords, and that "temporary" stack of mail that's lived there for three years. Hallways are where falls happen at their worst — during motion.
  • Lighting: A hallway dark enough to need a phone flashlight is a hazard. Nightlights with light sensors ($10–$15 each) solve it instantly.
  • A sturdy chair or console table near the entry for setting things down. Not a wobbly stool.

Living room

  • Cords and rugs: The two most common trip hazards in American homes. Tape cords along the wall (cord covers, not just painter's tape). Rugs that slide get non-slip pads or get retired; small rugs get removed entirely.
  • Furniture spacing: Can you walk from the couch to the kitchen without detouring like a skier? Aim for clear 32-inch paths (arms-width-plus).
  • Seating that works: A chair is friendly if your hips are level with (or above) your knees when you sit. Low, soft sofas are stylish and hostile. A firm cushion booster or furniture risers under the legs can fix a good chair that's too low.
  • Reachable shelves: The things you use most should live between waist and eye height. Climbing stepladders is the top cause of home injuries at 70+.

Kitchen

  • Zone check: Standing where you cook, everything you use daily should be reachable without bending below the counter or reaching on tiptoe to the top shelf. One weekend of re-shelving beats years of awkward stretching.
  • The oven-to-counter gap: A pull-out shelf or a rolling cart gives you a landing zone for hot dishes.
  • Heavy things low, light things high — reversal of the usual rule. Cast iron skillets go on mid shelves now.
  • Lighting: Under-cabinet LED strips turn counter shadows into daylight. Battery or plug-in versions need no electrician.
  • Stove safety knob covers or a simple timer you wear on a lanyard: burning the pot is a "tired day" problem, not a "careless person" problem. Design for tired days.
  • Water detector under the sink ($10–$25): catches a slow leak before it becomes cabinet rot or a slippery floor.

Bathroom — read this one twice

The bathroom produces the highest-velocity hazards in the house: hard surfaces, water, small spaces, and full-body balance moments.

  • Grab bars are not a luxury. We cover placement in Grab Bars & Lighting, but if you read one sentence: bars go in the tub/shower and beside the toilet, mounted into studs, and never as a substitute towel bar.
  • Tub and shower: Non-slip mat or textured strips. If getting over the tub wall is already hard, a transfer bench or a plumber-installed tub-insert cutout can change life for a few hundred dollars.
  • Toilet height: Standard toilets are 15 inches; many adults need 17–19 inches. An elevated toilet seat or comfort-height replacement is a small fix with outsized dignity value.
  • Shower head: A hand-held head on a slide rail lets a seated person shower without wrestling.
  • Night path: The 3 a.m. bathroom run causes a notable share of falls. Motion nightlights + a clear path + a reachable light switch (or a glow toggle by the bed) fix the whole route.
  • Mirror and shelf items at seated height if needed. A second "bathroom guest" setup (extra razors, soap, towels) in a caddy means fewer grab-and-slip moments.

Bedroom

  • Bed height: Hips level with knees when sitting on the edge. Bed risers or a lower frame can fix either direction.
  • Phone within arm's reach of the bed. Or a wearable call button. This is both the most common and most overlooked item in the guide.
  • Landing zone: A wide, stable lamp and somewhere to put glasses, water, and the book. Fumbling in the dark for a charging cable is a real fall setup.
  • Closet: Move daily-wear clothes to the reachable two-thirds of the closet. High shelves become "guest" or seasonal storage only.
  • Path from bed to door: Clear, night-lit, no roundabout through the foot of the bed.

Laundry and utility

  • Front-load or raised machines are easier than deep top-loaders. A platform under a top-loader, or a folding table at standing height, removes the worst bending.
  • Laundry caddy: One container to move wash → dry → fold without trips having to be made at each step.
  • Smoke/CO detector locations: Sleep in the same zone as the laundry? A plug-in rechargeable smoke detector might be overkill — but a door-closed laundry room vent + a working detector is non-negotiable.

Basement, garage, and outdoors

  • Stairs get handrails top and bottom, both sides if possible. Lighting with a switch at both ends.
  • Garage floor: Oil spots and a slope are slippery when wet. A degreaser weekend + a rubber-backed mat at the car door.
  • The "I'll get around to it" shelf: Basements accumulate sharp, heavy, high, and wobbly things. One weekend to re-home the dangerous stuff to safer spots; a label system ("Do not climb" shelf) beats memory.
  • Yard path: Hoses coiled, branches trimmed to head height, gates that latch without a wrestling match.

After the walk, total your notes and go to Fall-Risk Fixes in Weekend Doses.
