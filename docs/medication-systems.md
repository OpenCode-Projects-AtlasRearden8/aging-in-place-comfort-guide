# Medication & Appointment Systems That Survive Bad Days

*Administration help only — never medical advice.*

This page is about **organization**, not medicine. We never tell you what to take, when to change a dose, or how to manage a condition — that's your doctor and pharmacist. We tell you how to build a system that keeps working when your memory doesn't, or when you're having the kind of week where every day is Tuesday.

The design rule for everything below: **assume the bad day is normal.** A system built for your best day fails exactly when it's needed.

---

## The pain points, named

1. **Pills:** new prescriptions, refills running out mid-week, the "did I take it?" fog, pill bottles from three doctors living in three different rooms.
2. **Appointments:** a paper slip on the fridge, a card in a wallet, "was it Tuesday or Thursday?," and the drive that nobody arranged.
3. **Paperwork:** insurance letters, explanation-of-benefits envelopes, discharge papers from a hospital visit — a stack nobody reads.

Each gets its own system. They share one principle: **one place each, and the place is obvious.**

## Pill system (organizational only)

**Step 1 — One home base.** All medicine, vitamins, and supplements live in ONE kitchen cabinet or ONE drawer — not bedroom-and-kitchen split. Pick the place closest to the center of your morning routine (near the coffee maker works for most people).

**Step 2 — A weekly pill organizer.** The classic 7-day, twice-a-day box is a marvel: fill it once a week (a "fill day" — Sunday morning with the radio on, for many people), and daily becomes one lid, no bottles. Organizers come in large-lid, high-contrast versions — worth $15.

**Step 3 — The "did I take it?" switch.** Options in order of simplicity:

- Flip-the-bottle: take pills, turn the lid upside down. Zero tech.
- A card taped to the organizer with AM/PM checkboxes and a pencil on a string.
- A $10 e-ink or kitchen timer cube you press to log the dose.

Pick ONE. Two systems = two chances to contradict each other.

**Step 4 — Guard rails for refill day:**

- A phone reminder (with the sound ON) for the same weekday, e.g., "Sunday 10 a.m.: Fill pill box + check bottles."
- The rule of three: if a bottle has fewer than **seven days** of pills left, it goes in the refill pile. No judgment, just physics.
- Refill pile location: a small tray next to the pill box. Phone orders, mail-order, or pharmacy pickup — the tray is the trigger, the method doesn't matter.

**Step 5 — The one-page med list.** Write (or ask the pharmacist to print) a list of everything taken: name, dose, why, who prescribed it. Tape a copy inside the medicine cabinet, keep a copy in the wallet, and hand one to the person you'll list in [Family Conversations](family-conversations.md). Update it whenever something changes. This list is what an EMT asks for first.

> **Never change or stop medicines based on anything in this guide.** Changing plans goes through the prescriber. The organizer, reminders, and lists here are just logistics.

## Appointment system

**Step 1 — One calendar, one place.** A big wall calendar in the kitchen wins for low tech; a digital calendar (phone, or a simple shared calendar with a family member) wins for reminders that ring even on a messy day. Many households do both: wall calendar is the master view, phone pings the morning-of.

**Step 2 — The capture habit.** Every appointment goes in the calendar **the moment it's made** — while still on the phone with the receptionist. Not "later." The slip of paper from the doctor's office goes into a tray labeled TO ADD, and the calendar gets updated during a weekly 10-minute reset (same day as pill-box filling — pair the habits).

**Step 3 — Ride and prep for each entry.** For big items (especially specialist visits), add three notes to the calendar entry:

- **Ride:** who's driving / taxi / transit plan.
- **Bring:** med list, insurance card, ID, glasses, questions list.
- **Question:** one thing you want answered, written down beforehand — bad-day brains forget clever questions but a sentence in the calendar entry survives.

**Step 4 — Paper triage.** One box or drawer for insurance/medical mail. Weekly, during the same 10-minute reset: pitch the marketing, file the EOBs in the box (sorted or not — the box is fine), and act on real bills or notices by adding calendar entries. No perfect filing system needed; a consistent box beats an inconsistent system.

## The weekly 10-minute reset (the keystone habit)

One day a week (Sunday for most), 10 minutes, same time:

1. Fill the pill organizer; check the <7-day rule.
2. Update the calendar from the TO ADD tray.
3. Confirm this week's rides and bring-lists.
4. Toss expired stuff from the fridge door; refill the pill-taking pencil.

That's the whole system. It survives bad days because bad days don't need the system to be remembered — it resets itself weekly regardless of how the week went.

## If a family member is helping

Great — but see [Talking Family Through Changes](family-conversations.md) first, and the companion **Caregiver Coordination Guide** for shared task lists and visit schedules. The single most powerful move: give the helper **read access** to the calendar and the medication list, and tell them the update schedule is "Sundays." Then nobody calls in a panic about a schedule only one person holds in their head.
